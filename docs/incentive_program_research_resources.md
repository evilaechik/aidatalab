# Business Incentives Research: Atlanta and Peer Metros

**Project question:** Where is Atlanta lagging in business attraction and retention, what factors are associated with this, and what incentives could Atlanta implement or expand?

**Purpose of this brief:** Provide a sourced first-pass inventory of incentive programs and a research plan for Step 3. It identifies program approaches to investigate against the project's economic impact, jobs and employment, taxes and incentives, infrastructure, and housing/cost-of-living themes. It does not benchmark metro performance or infer that a program caused a project's location decision.

## Scope and data boundaries

The peer metros named for the project are Austin, Charlotte, Dallas, Denver, Houston, Nashville, Raleigh, and Miami. Atlanta is the home-market policy reference. ## What counts as attraction, expansion, and retention

Use one **project decision** as the unit of analysis and record the company's pre-project presence in the relevant geography.

| Decision type | Working definition | Recommended outcome measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attraction | A business new to the defined geography chooses to establish an operating location there. A new function or headquarters can be tracked as a sub-type when the company already has operations elsewhere in the region. | New operating locations; net new local jobs actually filled; actual payroll and wages; capital investment delivered; time to opening; survival after 2 and 5 years. |
| Expansion | An existing local business adds productive capacity, jobs, payroll, floor area, or investment beyond a documented baseline. A move between municipalities within one metro is a relocation within the metro, not automatically metro-level job growth. | Net additions versus pre-project baseline; filled jobs; wage/payroll growth; capex delivered; added space/capacity; continuation of operations. |
| Retention | An existing business makes a documented decision to keep an operation or jobs in the geography despite a credible alternative to close, shrink, or relocate. Ordinary continued operation is not enough to count as a retention win. | Jobs at risk and retained; retained payroll and investment; closure/relocation avoided with evidence; subsequent operation at 2/5 years. |

Keep **announced/committed** figures separate from **realized** results. Keep City of Atlanta separate from metro Atlanta and apply the same rule to every peer metro.

## Cross-cutting research frame

Map each incentive to the project factors and outcomes it is designed to affect. This is a classification framework for research, not a finding that the incentive works.

| Project theme | Incentive mechanisms to code | Outcomes / mediators to collect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic impact | Grants, loans, tax credits, tax abatements, TIF/TAD, small-business finance | Actual investment, local tax receipts, supplier spending, business survival, spillover jobs; distinguish gross from net public benefit. |
| Jobs and employment | Per-job grants, payroll/job credits, wage premiums, customized training, on-the-job training, incumbent-worker training, retention grants | Net new jobs, filled vs announced jobs, local resident hires, wage/benefits, training completion, retention after 1/2/5 years. |
| Taxes and incentives | Corporate/job credits, sales/use exemptions, property-tax abatements, refunds, performance grants | Awarded and paid value, effective tax benefit, public cost per realized job, duration, clawbacks, interaction/stacking. |
| Infrastructure and sites | Public infrastructure grants, TIF/TAD, site preparation, brownfield or utility support, transit/access improvements | Site readiness, time to permit/open, infrastructure capacity, access, public and private cost, delivery and utilization. |
| Housing and cost of living | Workforce/affordable housing finance, transit-oriented development, employer-linked housing support where legally available | Housing cost burden, commute times, worker recruitment/retention, proximity to jobs, housing units delivered. These are typically place/workforce interventions, not direct company subsidies. |

Atlanta's current program set already includes examples across several categories: job and training support, project-specific business assistance, district-based TAD financing, and workforce-housing finance. The next research task is to establish actual rules, current funding, usage, and outcomes, then assess whether a gap exists for the specific sector/project type where Atlanta's attraction or retention problem is documented.

## Research questions and workflow

### Core research question

**For business projects that Atlanta seeks to attract, expand, or retain, which program features—type, eligibility, target, and structure—address the documented barrier, and are they associated with additional, realized public benefits compared with similar projects that did not receive the incentive?**

### Research sequence

1. **Diagnose the gap before selecting a program.** Use the project's outcome data to identify a specific outcome, industry/firm segment, geography, and period. For example, define whether the concern is fewer new operations, weaker net job growth, losses among existing employers, or project delivery below commitments.
2. **Code the program, not just the announcement.** For each program, record administering level, source, effective dates, type, eligibility, target, award formula/cap, duration, required match, performance benchmarks, payment timing, compliance checks, clawbacks, stacking, and current funding availability.
3. **Code project treatment and decision timing.** Record the incentive applied for, offered, awarded, contracted, paid, and amount; distinguish no award from unknown. Record the firm’s location alternatives and whether it had committed to Atlanta before approval.
4. **Link to delivery.** Track openings, realized jobs and wages, capital investment, space, time to delivery, business survival, and cancellations. Compare every announced value with subsequent verified results.
5. **Measure non-incentive factors.** For the project’s industry and site, collect workforce supply/skill fit, wages, land/building costs and availability, infrastructure readiness, housing/commute access, utility costs, market access, and business-climate factors. Use company statements/interviews to confirm which factors actually entered the decision.
6. **Estimate contribution cautiously.** Begin with descriptive program use and delivery rates. For causal estimates, seek comparable eligible projects or policy changes and document pre-trends and selection. If a credible counterfactual is unavailable, report case evidence/associations rather than a causal incentive effect.

## Assumptions and verification flags

- The peer list follows the project brief. “Denver,” “Miami,” “Houston,” and other city labels may refer to city, county, metro, or state programs; record the administering jurisdiction, not just the metro name.
- Incentive pages are examples of programs and published rules, not evidence that a specific project received support. Obtain award agreements, board approvals, disbursement reports, or official annual reports to document actual awards.
- Program pages are dynamic. Verify current eligibility, caps, and appropriations at the time the analysis is finalized. In particular, Georgia's official incentive page currently reports a 2026 corporate income-tax rate of 4.99%; the existing repository baseline lists 5.19% and should be refreshed before performance or tax-context comparisons.
- WorkSource Atlanta currently states its customized-training funding is unavailable; availability should be checked again before proposing expansion.
- The linked Google Doc could not be accessed in this environment. This brief does not assert that its contents were reviewed.
- No effectiveness ranking or cross-metro performance comparison is made in this research resource.
