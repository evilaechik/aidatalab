# Incentives and Research Plan

## 3. Compare incentive strategies

### Where the outcome data points

The project’s recent Q4 QCEW comparison (2024–25) identifies Atlanta’s employment decline in transportation and warehousing (-23,424 jobs; -12.39%), manufacturing (-3,539; -2.02%), information (-2,760; -3.33%), and finance and insurance (-2,433; -1.94%). In the same period Austin grew in manufacturing (+12,836; +17.94%) and professional services (+5,738; +3.83%); Charlotte grew in finance and insurance (+4,592; +4.63%). These figures justify examining Austin’s manufacturing tools and Charlotte’s finance/job-growth tools as candidate comparators. They do not establish that either region’s incentive programs caused its growth. The underlying analysis is metro-wide, while most incentives are administered at state, county, or city level.

### Program design comparison

| Region / program | Type and eligibility | Targeting and structure | Relevance to Atlanta’s observed gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Atlanta / Georgia: Job Tax Credits, Quality Jobs Tax Credit, manufacturing and distribution exemptions** | State tax credits/exemptions for qualifying new jobs and business inputs. Quality Jobs credits apply to net-new jobs paying at least 110% of the county average wage; manufacturing sales/use tax exemptions cover qualifying machinery, materials and essential energy. A distribution facility may qualify for an equipment exemption with at least $5 million in investment. | Primarily industry, wage and investment eligibility. Tax credits offset corporate income tax and, where rules permit, payroll withholding; they are not the same as an upfront project grant. Georgia’s official page reports a 4.99% corporate income-tax rate for 2026. | Georgia already has manufacturing and distribution-oriented tax tools, so Atlanta’s sector decline is not evidence of a missing incentive category. The next question is whether metro Atlanta projects know, qualify for, claim, and retain these benefits—and whether other constraints (sites, labor, logistics costs or market conditions) explain the decline. |
| **Austin / Texas: Texas Enterprise Fund (TEF)** | Competitive, discretionary cash grant for a new facility or expansion where a Texas location is still competing with at least one viable out-of-state site. Current eligibility requires more than 75 new full-time jobs in urban areas (25 rural), average new-job wages at least equal to the county average, significant capital investment, local support, financial soundness, and an advanced industry with other location options. | Deal-closing support for projects with documented site competition. Award model weighs new jobs, average wages, hiring schedule and capital investment. Payments are made after contract and satisfaction of job/wage targets, typically annually; contracts can require repayment for underperformance. Separately, Texas Skills Development Fund grants support customized training through public community or technical colleges. | This design is more tightly tied to a documented competitive location decision and verified delivery than a broad tax preference. It is a useful model to test for large manufacturing or advanced-industry projects, but the employment data alone cannot establish Austin TEF participation or program effect. For a skills bottleneck, compare Texas’s college/employer-led training with Atlanta’s WorkSource options rather than treating training grants as location subsidies. |
| **Charlotte / North Carolina: JDIG and OneNC** | JDIG is a discretionary performance grant for new or expanding firms, based on payroll withholding from net-new jobs. OneNC is a separate negotiated cash grant routed through local government for competitive projects; county-average wage, health insurance, out-of-state competition, and local match are among its basic conditions. | JDIG award calculations account for net jobs, wages relative to county average, investment, targeted industry and county tier. Annual payments can run up to 12 years under program rules. County tier affects how much stays with the firm versus the state utility account. OneNC payments are staged as job targets are met and require a local match; companies must maintain at least 90% of jobs for a defined period after the grant. | This structure aligns with finance-sector expansion by linking payment to net job and wage delivery, while directing a share of support toward less prosperous counties. It offers a candidate comparison for Charlotte’s finance growth, but a statewide award to a company in another North Carolina county is not proof that the Charlotte metro received it or that it created the metro’s growth. |
| **Atlanta / City: EOF, Middle-Wage Job Fund and WorkSource Atlanta** | Invest Atlanta’s Economic Opportunity Fund is discretionary, project-by-project assistance for businesses or nonprofits; the published criteria include job creation/retention, business financial strength, investment, and eligible project costs. The Middle-Wage Job Fund has also funded resident training and job placement through grants to partner organizations. WorkSource Atlanta provides workforce services; its OJT and customized-training offers are participant- and funding-dependent. | Local and project-specific; possible support includes eligible acquisition/construction, equipment, infrastructure, leasehold improvements, relocation or training costs. WorkSource OJT reimburses eligible training wages, while customized training may reimburse up to 50% of eligible training costs; current availability varies, and its customized-training page currently says funding is unavailable. | Atlanta has a mix of business assistance and workforce tools. The potential structural difference is that peer state programs make attraction awards explicitly contingent on competing locations and measurable delivery, while Atlanta’s city programs can address resident hiring, job quality and project-specific costs. A fair comparison must include program-level funding, approval and payment records, not just program descriptions. |

### What the listed datasets can test

| Listed measure | Use in this comparison | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| QCEW employment; CBP employment and establishments | Track sector employment and establishment change for metro/county comparisons; use establishment counts to distinguish broad firm presence from large-employer job swings. | They do not identify incentive recipients, announced versus realized project jobs, or whether a business received an award. Match consistent NAICS, geography and years. |
| Economic Census revenue; BEA county GDP | Describe business scale, sector output/revenue, and changes in local economic production alongside employment. | Revenue/GDP growth is not an incentive outcome by itself and may reflect prices, productivity, or industry composition. Economic Census years and GDP series are not annual project follow-up measures. |
| State corporate income-tax rates | Contextualize one part of state tax policy. | A statutory rate does not measure effective taxes, credits claimed, sales/property taxes, or negotiated project incentives. The current 2026 rate field should be refreshed from the official source before comparison. |
| Fortune 500; CBP establishments | Identify major-company presence and business base as context for project attraction/retention. | Headquarters rankings and establishment counts do not show local incentive use, job quality, or causal effects. |
| BDS Metro openings and closures | Compare business formation and closure patterns as broad dynamism/retention indicators. | Metro aggregates cannot identify supported firms or distinguish incentive effects from economic cycles and firm composition. |

### Comparison conclusion

The outcome evidence supports a focused follow-up: (1) test manufacturing and logistics-related project-level outcomes against Austin/Texas eligibility and training structures; (2) test finance-sector job and establishment outcomes against Charlotte/North Carolina’s performance grant design; and (3) audit Georgia and Atlanta program uptake, award amounts and delivery in the same sectors. The listed datasets establish the outcome and context side of that comparison, but not the program-treatment side. Add a program-version table and a project-level file recording application, offer, award, payment, eligibility, jobs/wages/investment commitments, verified results, geography, and clawbacks before estimating whether incentives are associated with stronger outcomes.

### Recommended approach for a defensible comparison

Treat this as a **two-stage diagnostic and program evaluation**, not as a contest to identify the city with the biggest incentive. The first stage confirms which Atlanta outcomes are weak and chooses relevant peers. The second tests whether differences in program design and use plausibly address those outcomes.

#### 1. Define the lag before choosing the peer

Use metro-level outcomes for the initial screen and define a fixed period (preferably a 3–5 year trend, with 2024–25 as a recent signal rather than the sole decision basis). For each sector, calculate:

- QCEW employment level, absolute change, percent change, and share of total private employment.
- CBP establishment counts and size classes, to see whether job changes reflect many firms or a few large facilities.
- Economic Census revenue and BEA GDP/output measures as scale and production context, not substitutes for job outcomes.
- BDS openings, expansions, contractions, and closures where available, to distinguish attraction/formation from retention and contraction.

Compare Atlanta with peers using both absolute and percentage changes; Atlanta’s larger base can produce larger job counts without faster growth. Align years, NAICS definitions, metro boundaries, and private/public coverage. Mark missing or suppressed values explicitly. A sector becomes a priority only when the direction is persistent or corroborated by more than one outcome (for example, declining employment plus declining establishments), or when a clear strategic objective supports it.

#### 2. Select peers by the same sector and project type

Do not call a region a “top performer” based on all-sector growth or a single year. Choose peers separately for each Atlanta gap based on comparable sector growth, project activity, and metro role. The current evidence suggests Austin as a **candidate** for manufacturing and Charlotte as a **candidate** for finance; it does not yet establish either as the best comparator for Atlanta logistics. Add metros only after running the same sector screen. For each candidate, note the size of the sector, recent trend, business openings/closures, and major project concentration before inspecting programs.

Keep city and metro geography separate. QCEW, CBP and GDP comparisons are metro/county measures; a city incentive should only be linked to a project when the actual address and administering jurisdiction are verified. Statewide rules may be relevant to metro projects, but state awards must not be described as city awards.

#### 3. Compare the program mechanism to the diagnosed barrier

Code one program version per row, and compare like with like. A workforce reimbursement should not be ranked against a corporate tax credit as if they were interchangeable. First assign each program to the mechanism it intends to address:

| Diagnosed barrier / outcome | Program features to compare | Evidence of a plausible fit |
|---|---|---|
| Net job creation or wage quality | Per-job/payroll credit, performance grant, wage floor, job threshold, term, maintenance period | Verified net jobs and wages above a defined baseline; sustained employment after final payment |
| Manufacturing or distribution investment | Equipment/material tax exemption, capital grant, site-readiness or infrastructure support | Verified eligible capex, facility opening, capacity, and direct/indirect jobs; include public cost and timing |
| Labor supply or skill mismatch | OJT, customized training, college/employer partnership, incumbent-worker upskilling | Enrollments, completions, credentials, placement, wage change, retention and employer cost share |
| Firm formation and retention | Small-business finance, expansion assistance, layoff-aversion or retention services | Business survival, openings/closures, local ownership, employment and follow-up duration |
| Place or access constraints | TIF/TAD, site assembly, utility, transit or housing support | Site delivery, occupancy, commute/access change, housing units, and who benefits; do not label as a firm incentive without project linkage |

For every program, record: jurisdiction/administering agency; effective dates; eligible applicant and project; sector and place targeting; minimum jobs, wage and investment; application timing and site-competition test; award formula and cap; duration; match; funding source; payment milestones; verification; clawback/recapture; stacking; appropriation/available balance; and source date. Separate statutory maximums from actual awards and payments.

#### 4. Build a project-level evidence file

The incentive comparison requires a new project dataset because none of the listed economic datasets identify incentive recipients. Start with official announcements, board/council agendas, executed agreements, annual reports, audits and public-record award/payment tables. For each attraction, expansion, retention, closure or relocation decision, capture:

`project_id`, company, NAICS/sector, site address, city/county/metro/state, decision type, announcement and opening dates, program/version, application/offer/award/contract/payment dates, requested/awarded/paid/recaptured dollars, promised jobs/wages/capex, verified jobs/wages/capex at 1/2/5 years, jobs maintained, local/resident hires if disclosed, performance conditions, status, and source URLs.

Use distinct values for **no incentive documented**, **confirmed no incentive**, and **unknown**. Link each project to its underlying program rule and retain the original announcement separately from follow-up outcomes. Publicly announced jobs and investment are commitments, not realized results.

#### 5. Measure program use and delivery before estimating effects

For each region and program type, first report program availability, eligible project counts, applications if public, awards, dollars committed, dollars paid, verified completion, clawbacks, and missing outcome coverage. Then calculate:

- Delivery rate = verified jobs (or investment) / promised jobs (or investment), using a consistent observation date.
- Public cost per verified net job = dollars paid / verified net jobs, with project and cohort dates stated.
- Wage premium and retention = realized wage versus local/sector baseline, and jobs retained after the award period.
- Business dynamism = openings, expansions, contractions and closures among supported firms versus the metro baseline.

Present cohorts by award year and years since award; avoid comparing a recently announced project with an older completed cohort. Report medians and distributions as well as totals so one mega-project does not determine the result. If award dollars or verified delivery cannot be obtained, state that the comparison is limited to program design and announced commitments.

#### 6. Use an evaluation ladder and keep claims proportional

1. **Descriptive comparison:** Are incentives available and used in the target sector? What are the eligibility and payment differences?
2. **Delivery review:** Did funded projects meet commitments, and what public cost was paid per verified result?
3. **Matched project comparison:** Compare funded projects with similar non-funded/runner-up projects in the same sector, period and geography, controlling for scale, wages, investment, firm size and site conditions.
4. **Causal evaluation only when feasible:** Use a documented policy change or a credible eligibility threshold/design with enough pre/post observations and comparable trends. Account for selection into awards and location choice.

With only three metros and aggregate employment, a regression of metro performance on incentive programs would not identify program effects. The initial result should be framed as: “program features are consistent/inconsistent with the diagnosed need” or “supported projects delivered X,” not “the incentive caused regional growth,” unless a credible counterfactual supports the latter.

#### Initial testable questions

- **Manufacturing:** Are Austin’s positive manufacturing employment and establishment trends broad-based or driven by a few large plants? Which projects received TEF, local incentives, or customized training, and what portion of committed jobs/capex was delivered?
- **Finance and insurance:** Do Charlotte’s employment and establishment gains coincide with JDIG/OneNC-supported projects, and are their verified jobs/wages higher than comparable non-supported projects? How much of the trend is outside Charlotte’s city limits?
- **Atlanta manufacturing/logistics:** Are Georgia’s existing job credits and equipment exemptions used by comparable projects? Are the observed declines concentrated among establishments that closed, contracted, relocated, or cut headcount? Are site availability, labor, freight access or utility capacity cited in decisions?
- **Across programs:** Do performance-conditioned cash grants deliver more verified jobs per public dollar than tax credits, after adjusting for project scale, wage quality, geography, and time to payment? This is a hypothesis to test, not an assumption.

#### Suggested deliverables

1. A one-page sector benchmark selecting the priority gaps and justified peers.
2. A program-rule comparison matrix with sources, effective dates, and the four requested dimensions: type, eligibility, targeting and structure.
3. A project-award and delivery dataset, including missingness and geography checks.
4. A results dashboard or short memo separating commitments, payments and realized outcomes.
5. A recommendation only after the evidence identifies a specific design gap (for example, training access, site readiness, wage threshold, or performance verification) that Atlanta’s current programs do not address.

## Incentive-related variables currently available

The baseline has one incentive-related field: `state_corporate_tax_context_2026`. It describes broad state corporate tax treatment. It is useful as **policy context**, but it is not a company-specific incentive award or a complete measure of what a business pays.

| Current field | What it can support | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `state_corporate_tax_context_2026` | Record state corporate-tax treatment as one part of the business-cost environment. | Which firm or project qualifies; actual effective tax paid; whether an incentive was offered, accepted, or caused a location decision. |

The current tables do **not** contain program-level fields for:

- Program type, such as tax credit, grant, loan, workforce training, site or infrastructure support, or property-tax relief.
- Eligibility thresholds, such as minimum job count, wage, investment, industry, or location requirements.
- Targeting, including priority sectors, distressed areas, small businesses, or strategic facilities.
- Structure, including award amount, duration, reimbursement schedule, performance milestones, clawbacks, stacking rules, or funding source.
- Project-specific incentive amounts offered, negotiated, awarded, paid, or recaptured.

These are gaps to research; they should not be filled by assuming that a project announcement received a particular incentive.

## Suggested research question

**How are specific business-incentive program features associated with the attraction, expansion, retention, and realized outcomes of business projects in Atlanta, and how do those relationships vary by project type, industry, geography, and firm eligibility?**

This is an association question until the research design can address selection and causality. Companies and governments do not receive or offer incentives at random: projects receiving incentives may differ from those that do not.

Useful subquestions:

1. Which incentive types are used for attraction, expansion, retention, or redevelopment projects?
2. Which companies and projects are eligible, and what sectors, locations, wage levels, job counts, and investment thresholds are targeted?
3. How are awards structured, and what performance conditions apply?
4. What share of announced jobs, investment, and space is later realized, delayed, reduced, or cancelled?
5. Which project characteristics and stated location factors are associated with receiving an incentive and with later delivery?
6. What role do market scale, workforce, costs, sites, infrastructure, and customer access play alongside incentives in firm location decisions?

## Recommended data collection and analysis

### 1. Define the unit and geography

Use one row per **project decision** (not one row per company or one row per press release). Link follow-up announcements to that project. Record the exact address and jurisdiction and classify each observation as City of Atlanta, metro Atlanta outside the city, or outside the metro. Apply the same city/metro distinction to peer observations.

### 2. Add a sourced incentive-program table

Create a separate table with one row per program and version/effective period. Recommended fields:

| Field | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| `program_name`, `administering_entity`, `source_url` | Official name, government or authority, and primary documentation. |
| `program_type` | Tax credit, grant, loan, training, site/infrastructure, property-tax relief, or other documented support. |
| `effective_start`, `effective_end` | Dates the rules apply. |
| `eligible_entity`, `eligible_project`, `eligible_industries` | Who or what may qualify, using the source's actual terms. |
| `eligible_geography` | City, county, state, zone, or other defined area. |
| `threshold_jobs`, `threshold_wage`, `threshold_investment` | Published minimums or criteria; keep unknown values blank. |
| `award_formula`, `maximum_award`, `award_duration` | Published calculation, caps, and term. |
| `performance_conditions`, `clawback_terms`, `stacking_rules` | Required delivery, repayment/recapture, and combination rules. |
| `funding_source`, `decision_authority` | Public entity paying and entity approving the award. |

Keep program rules separate from project-level awards. A program's maximum award is not evidence that a particular firm received that amount.

### 3. Link projects to incentive actions and realized results

Add fields to the project table for `program_id`, `incentive_offered`, `incentive_requested`, `incentive_awarded`, `incentive_paid`, `incentive_amount`, `award_date`, and evidence URLs. Record a documented `no_incentive_found` separately from `unknown`; absence of a public announcement is not proof that no incentive existed.

Add follow-up measures for actual jobs, wages, investment, space, opening date, and project status at consistent intervals. Preserve original announced values and dates instead of overwriting them with realized values. Include cancelled and withdrawn projects.

### 4. Start with descriptive analysis

First summarize incentive use by program type, eligibility, target, project type, industry, geography, and year. Then report announced versus realized results, missingness, cancellations, and source coverage. Do not call these differences incentive impacts.

### 5. Evaluate effects only if the data support it

For a causal question, identify a credible comparison group and policy timing. Possible designs may include a matched project comparison or difference-in-differences around a program change, but use them only if treatment and comparison projects have adequate pre/post observations and comparable trends. Account for sector, site, project size, workforce and market conditions, and jurisdictions' selection of projects. If those conditions are not met, present descriptive associations and case evidence only.

## What can be said about Atlanta's business appeal from the present fields

The baseline contains indicators of Atlanta's **market scale**: establishments, private employment, aggregate wages, and GDP. These can help describe the existing business ecosystem and economic base. The decision tracker also records project functions, industries, locations, and announced scale for a limited set of public announcements.

The current data cannot determine which factor makes a firm choose Atlanta. They do not measure company-reported reasons consistently, nor do they contain comparable measures of talent availability, operating costs, real estate, sites, infrastructure, logistics, customer access, or incentive offers. Market scale is a plausible factor to investigate, not a demonstrated cause. To answer the business-location question, collect company statements or filings and compare them with measurable site, workforce, cost, market-access, and incentive attributes for both selected and non-selected locations.

## Assumptions and limits

- The prompt's table path was a placeholder, so this plan uses the two public repository tables listed above.
- The baseline is metro-level while the tracker distinguishes city and metro project locations. Do not attribute a suburban metro project to the City of Atlanta.
- The baseline covers different measurement periods: 2025 employment-related metrics, 2023–24 GDP growth, and 2026 tax context. Align periods before modeling.
- Announced project values are not realized outcomes. The tracker is an initial public-source sample, not a complete project census.
- The current state-tax field is not an effective-tax calculation or a complete incentive inventory.
- No region-level or program-level effectiveness conclusion is made here.
