# Step 3: Descriptive analysis for the incentive research question

## Research question

**How are specific business-incentive program features associated with the attraction, expansion, retention, and realized outcomes of business projects in Atlanta, and how do those relationships vary by project type, industry, geography, and firm eligibility?**

This is a descriptive analysis of the project’s currently available public tables. It identifies outcome patterns and useful project leads. The available tables do not contain incentive application, offer, award, payment, or clawback data, so they cannot estimate an incentive effect or show that a listed company received public support.

## 1. Regional scale and recent overall performance

| Metro | Private employment (2025) | Employment change (2025) | Calculated growth | Establishments (2025) | GDP (2024, $B) | GDP growth (2023–24) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Atlanta | 2,533,698 | +3,049 | +0.12% | 229,977 | 604.3 | +5.4% |
| Austin | 1,113,111 | +38,825 | +3.49% | 75,886 | 268.4 | +6.6% |
| Charlotte | 1,190,963 | +12,705 | +1.07% | 94,544 | 277.1 | +6.9% |

Atlanta has about 2.3 times Austin’s and 2.1 times Charlotte’s private employment, and the largest establishment base. Its 2025 employment growth was substantially slower: approximately 0.1%, compared with 3.5% in Austin and 1.1% in Charlotte. GDP growth is from a different period and measures output, not jobs; both peer metros grew faster than Atlanta on that measure as well.

**Interpretation:** Atlanta’s issue is not a small economic base. The recent signal is slower marginal job growth relative to the existing base. That supports looking at expansion, contraction, and retention outcomes alongside attraction. A single year is too short to establish a persistent competitiveness gap, and overall metro growth does not identify the cause.

Manufacturing, finance, information, and professional services also weakened or were nearly flat in Atlanta. The opposite health-care result is a reminder that Atlanta is not uniformly lagging. Avoid dividing the sector QCEW job change by the separate peer-baseline employment figure until the two sources’ totals and coverage are reconciled.

**Priorities for incentive follow-up:** (1) diagnose what drove transportation/warehousing losses before selecting an incentive comparator; (2) compare manufacturing programs and projects with Austin; (3) compare finance-sector expansion and grants with Charlotte; and (4) use information and professional services as secondary comparisons. These are candidate comparisons from one year of data, not final rankings.

## 3. Public project-announcement tracker

The tracker contains 15 rows: Atlanta 6, Austin 6, Charlotte 3. It records 3,205 announced jobs for Atlanta, 5,975 for Austin, and 7,800 for Charlotte. It records known capital investment of $131 million in Atlanta (2 rows), none for Austin (0 rows with a reported investment amount), and $1.267 billion in Charlotte (3 rows). These are announcement totals only and must not be compared as verified outcomes.

Important data-quality and interpretation notes:

- The counts span announcement years 2020–2026 and mix newly announced projects with older announcements. Cohort and follow-up time are not comparable.
- Charlotte’s 6,000-job Centene entry is explicitly marked cancelled after announcement. It contributes to announced totals but should be excluded from active commitments and analyzed as a cancellation.
- Compal in Austin appears in two industry rows (advanced manufacturing and information technology) with 900 and 100 announced jobs. Confirm whether these are separate commitments before counting projects or summing jobs; the same source/decision may be represented twice.
- Investment is missing for most records. A missing amount is unknown, not zero. The reported investment totals are incomplete.
- The tracker contains no incentive program or amount fields. An announcement, job count, or capital investment does not establish whether an incentive was offered, accepted, paid, or influential in the decision.
- Location scope differs. Atlanta records include city and metro projects; do not treat metro projects as City of Atlanta wins. The same project-address check is needed for peers.

The sample can seed case research (e.g., verify project status, address, awards, and realization), but it is not a complete project census and cannot support a peer-level win-rate ranking.

## 4. What the current data says about the big question

| Research dimension | Finding from available data | Evidence still needed |
|---|---|---|
| Attraction | Tracker has a few announced attraction cases in each metro, but coverage and time periods differ. | Comparable project census, site alternatives, rejected/lost deals, project status and incentive actions. |
| Expansion/retention | QCEW captures net sector employment change, not whether firms expanded, contracted, closed, or moved. Tracker has selected expansions but few follow-ups. | Firm establishment histories, closures/contractions, expansion projects, employer statements and retention agreements. |
| Industry and geography | Sector trends identify candidate gaps; project records distinguish some city from metro locations. | Consistent NAICS/industry coding, geocoded sites and comparable city/county/metro boundaries. |
| Program type, eligibility, targeting, structure | Official program rules can be compared from program sources, but no listed outcome dataset records who qualified or used a program. | Versioned program inventory plus project-to-program links, applications, offers, awards, payments, conditions and recapture. |
| Realized results | QCEW/CBP/GDP/BDS provide broad aggregate outcomes, while tracker values are announced commitments. | Project-level annual follow-up on jobs, wages, investment, openings, survival and public payments. |
| Causal contribution | Cannot be identified from these three metros or aggregate indicators. | Comparable treated/untreated projects or a credible policy change/design, adequate observations and pre-trend checks. |

The strongest responsible conclusion now is: **Atlanta entered the latest measured year with a much larger business base, but much slower total employment growth and pronounced sector weakness in transportation/warehousing, manufacturing, finance, and information. This makes program-level review in those areas relevant. The current data do not establish that incentive policy explains the differences.**

## 5. Next analysis steps

1. Extend sector comparisons to at least 2019–2025 (and earlier if consistent) using QCEW; compute annual and multi-year employment growth, establishment changes, and sector shares for the same metro definitions.
2. Use CBP to split changes by establishment size, and BDS Metro to distinguish openings, expansions, contractions, and closures. This will show whether weak net jobs reflect fewer firms, large-employer changes, or both.
3. Reconcile tracker duplicates and cancellation status; add NAICS, exact site, city/county/metro, and follow-up status. Keep announcements and later verified results as separate fields.
4. Build a program-award table for Atlanta/Georgia, Austin/Texas, and Charlotte/North Carolina: rules by version, applicants, offers, awards, paid amounts, annual verified jobs/wages/investment, compliance and clawbacks.
5. Compare projects within matched sector, decision type, size, year, wage level, and site type. Begin with descriptive delivery rates and public cost per verified job; only move to causal claims if a credible comparison and sufficient project observations exist.
