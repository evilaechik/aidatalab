# Atlanta Business Attraction: Research Guide

**Purpose:** A public-source reading list for researching what attracts large companies to Atlanta, what may constrain attraction or retention, and what evidence to collect next. This is a research guide, not a conclusion that Atlanta is succeeding or lagging on every factor.

## Main factors to investigate

1. **Workforce fit and cost:** occupation supply, credentials, wage levels, hiring difficulty, training capacity, and commute access.
2. **Sites and utilities:** suitable office/industrial sites, building readiness, land assembly, power/water/broadband capacity, utility cost, and delivery timelines.
3. **Logistics and market access:** freight reliability, airport/rail/highway access, customer and supplier proximity, and transportation cost.
4. **Industry clusters and suppliers:** whether firms can find customers, vendors, research partners, and specialized workers already in the region.
5. **Operating costs and predictability:** real estate, labor, utilities, taxes, permitting time, and regulatory certainty by industry and facility type.
6. **Quality of life and housing:** housing availability/affordability, commute burden, and ability to recruit and retain workers.
7. **Incentive design and accountability:** who qualifies, what benefit is offered, what the firm must deliver, whether the incentive changed the decision, and whether outcomes were realized.
8. **Retention and expansion:** local-company expansion, contraction, closure, relocation, and reasons for those decisions.

## Applying this to the repository data

The public repository data can help describe employment, establishments, wages, sector mix, GDP, and a sample of announced projects. It does not by itself measure skills availability, site readiness, utility capacity, housing affordability, complete incentive awards, company decision reasons, or realized project outcomes. Use those as explicit research gaps and add public, sourced measures before drawing conclusions. Keep city-level project locations distinct from metro- or county-level economic statistics.

## Assumptions and limitations

- “Atlanta” may mean the City of Atlanta or the wider metro. Keep the geographic scope explicit for every measure.
- “Large companies” can refer to headquarters, major offices, manufacturing plants, distribution centers, R&D facilities, or large expansions; their location criteria differ.
- Industry association, growth, and announcements do not establish causation. Company interviews, site-selection records, or credible evaluation designs are needed to assess why a specific location was chosen.
- The Chamber, CBRE, Invest Atlanta, and Area Development sources have organizational or industry perspectives. Pair them with public statistics and independent research.
- This file uses public sources and public repository materials only. No private-use data or restricted folders were consulted.
