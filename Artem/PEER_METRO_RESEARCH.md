# Atlanta, Austin, and Charlotte: first peer-metro baseline

Date: 2026-10-08

## Purpose

This is the first public-data baseline for the project question: where does Atlanta perform differently from peer metros in business attraction, expansion, and retention, and what factors could plausibly explain those differences?

**Geography:** the outcome of interest is the City of Atlanta. The figures below use metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) to compare regional business ecosystems. They cannot, by themselves, tell us whether the City rather than a suburban municipality received a job or an office.

## Working definitions

| Term | What it means | Best evidence | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attraction | A firm enters the metro or opens a new establishment there. | Announced relocation/opening, new establishment, new-to-market jobs and capital investment. | Overall job growth alone. |
| Expansion | An existing local firm adds jobs, space, capital investment, or a new local function. | Company announcement, permit/project record, local jobs/capital investment. | A larger company-wide revenue number. |
| Retention | An existing local employer stays, renews/expands, or avoids a planned departure. | Retention project records, lease/site decision, jobs retained, closure/relocation data. | A single year's employment level. |

Employment, establishments, wages, GDP, taxes, Fortune 500 headquarters, and office locations are **indicators or explanatory context**. They are not direct measures of all three outcomes.

## How to use the measures your group listed

| Measure | Use it for | Limitation | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Employment | Size and year-to-year direction of the regional business base; industry specialization. | It combines attraction, expansion, closures, retention, and labor-supply changes. | Core baseline metric. |
| Revenue | Scale of a local establishment only when the same reporting rule exists in all metros. | Private-company revenue is usually not public and company-wide revenue cannot be assigned to one office. | Do not use as a peer-metro headline measure. |
| GDP | Economic scale and broad growth. | City GDP is not consistently published; current-dollar growth includes inflation. | Use metro GDP derived from county data, with these caveats. |
| State corporate income tax | A broad cost/operating-environment factor. | A firm's actual tax burden also depends on apportionment, credits, local taxes, and Texas's margin tax. | Context only; do not treat it as the cause of a location decision. |
| Fortune 500 | Corporate-headquarters ecosystem and recognizable-company presence. | Headquarters count does not measure local offices, jobs, or expansions. | Supplementary context. |
| Company offices | City versus suburban capture of major-employer activity. | There is no universal public office-count database; a consistent company sample and address verification are required. | Direct project outcome metric once a research protocol is set. |

## Comparable public data

| Metro | 2025 private establishments | 2025 private employment | 2025 employment change | 2024 nominal GDP | 2023–24 nominal GDP growth | 2026 state corporate-tax context |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Atlanta | 229,977 | 2,533,698 | +3,049 (+0.12%) | $604.3B | +5.4% | Georgia: 5.19% corporate income tax |
| Austin | 75,886 | 1,113,111 | +38,825 (+3.61%) | $268.4B | +6.6% | No corporate income tax; Texas franchise-margin tax applies |
| Charlotte | 94,544 | 1,190,963 | +12,705 (+1.08%) | $277.1B | +6.9% | North Carolina: 2.00% corporate income tax |

### What the figures say

Atlanta has the largest regional private-sector base by a wide margin. It did **not** match Austin or Charlotte's 2025 private-employment growth rate. That is a signal worth investigating, not proof that Atlanta is losing attraction or retention decisions: overall employment also moves with local business cycles, sector mix, and labor supply.

GDP is shown in current dollars, so its growth rate includes inflation and cannot be used as a real-growth comparison without a price adjustment. Beginning in 2026, BEA discontinued publishing official MSA GDP directly; the figures above sum the official 2024 county GDP values across each MSA's counties.

## Sector pattern: private employment in 2025

| Sector | Atlanta | Austin | Charlotte | Initial interpretation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Information | 2.9% | 3.8% | 1.9% | Austin has the strongest information-employment share of the three. |
| Finance and insurance | 4.8% | 5.1% | 8.6% | Charlotte's finance concentration is its clearest advantage. |
| Professional, scientific, and technical services | 9.0% | 13.9% | 7.6% | Austin has a much larger professional-services share. |
| Management of companies and enterprises | 2.9% | 1.6% | 3.4% | Atlanta and Charlotte have stronger corporate-management footprints. |
| Manufacturing | 6.1% | 7.6% | 8.6% | Both peers have higher manufacturing shares. |
| Transportation and warehousing | 6.4% | 2.9% | 6.1% | Atlanta's logistics base is a clear strength. |
| Health care and social assistance | 14.3% | 12.3% | 10.7% | Atlanta has the largest health-care share. |

### Early hypotheses to test

1. **Austin:** Its advantage appears concentrated in technology-adjacent professional services and manufacturing. Test whether workforce supply, university/research ties, venture activity, industrial sites, and state tax structure explain firm-location decisions.
2. **Charlotte:** Its finance and corporate-management concentrations suggest a mature headquarters and financial-services ecosystem. Test whether specialized talent, industry networks, airport access, office inventory, and state tax structure support attraction and retention.
3. **Atlanta:** Atlanta has an unusually broad base, with particular strength in logistics, health care, and corporate management. The question is whether the City is capturing the office and innovation jobs generated by that regional ecosystem, or whether those decisions land mainly in suburban jurisdictions.

### Factor analysis at this stage

| Factor | Evidence in the baseline | What it suggests | What would confirm it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Industry cluster | Austin has the highest professional-services and information shares; Charlotte has the highest finance and corporate-management shares; Atlanta leads these peers in logistics and health-care shares. | Firms may follow specialized talent, suppliers, and peer firms already present in a metro. | Occupation data, degree completions, company move announcements, and interviews/press coverage that name talent or cluster access. |
| Recent labor-market momentum | Austin added 3.61% private employment in 2025; Charlotte added 1.08%; Atlanta added 0.12%. | Austin and Charlotte had stronger overall momentum during that year. | Separate attraction, expansion, closure, and retention records by year and industry. |
| State tax setting | Georgia's corporate rate is 5.19%; North Carolina's is 2.00%; Texas has no corporate income tax but does levy a margin tax. | Tax structure may affect total operating cost, especially for some corporate functions. | Comparable effective-tax scenarios for a representative office, logistics site, and manufacturing site after incentives. |
| City capture versus metro strength | The public baseline is metro-wide, while Invest Atlanta acts within City limits. | A strong Atlanta metro can still produce weak City office or job capture. | Geocoded office/facility addresses and project locations classified as City, metro suburb, or outside the metro. |

The evidence currently supports these as **testable explanations**, not causal findings. The next round of data should determine which factors firms actually cite when choosing or expanding sites.

## What to collect next

1. **Direct outcome data:** Invest Atlanta project records, state/local announcements, permits, and company announcements. Tag every record as attraction, expansion, retention, closure, or relocation; record jobs, capital investment, industry, date, and exact location.
2. **City-versus-suburb location data:** verified office/facility addresses for a defined sample of major employers. Track the City of Atlanta separately from metro-suburban locations.
3. **Factor data:** commercial rent/vacancy and available sites; transit and commute access; occupation and degree supply; housing costs; incentive awards and eligibility rules; utility costs; and sector-specific supply-chain assets.

## Source notes

- **Employment, establishments, and wages:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, 2025 annual county files. Values were summed across the counties in each 2023 Census delineated MSA, for private ownership and total private industry (NAICS 10). The 2025 annual files provide an over-the-year change field.
- **MSA county membership:** U.S. Census Bureau, July 2023 CBSA delineation file. Atlanta: 29 counties; Austin: 5; Charlotte: 11.
- **GDP:** U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, CAGDP1 county GDP, current-dollar GDP, 2023–2024. County values were summed using the same MSA county membership.
- **Tax context:** Tax Foundation, 2026 State Corporate Income Tax Rates and Brackets. State tax policy is a contextual factor; it does not identify the tax cost of a specific firm or site.
